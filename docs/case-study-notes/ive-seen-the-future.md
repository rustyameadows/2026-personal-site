# I’ve Seen the Future case study notes

Status: Source reconnaissance and focused interview complete. Full draft in `docs/case-study-drafts/ive-seen-the-future.md` approved by Rusty on 2026-08-13; not yet published.

These are working editorial notes for Rusty Meadows's personal I’ve Seen the Future case study. The story covers the enduring I’ve Seen the Future umbrella brand and Interpolations as its first public activation. The ISTF Lab is explicitly out of scope and must not inform the narrative, metadata, or visual edit.

## Rusty's opening account

I’ve Seen the Future is Rusty's high-level personal brand for exploring technology and the future of the world. It grows out of a lifelong love of learning, explaining, and sharing technology, and a personal ambition to host a World's Fair one day.

Rusty cites Richard Feynman and Carl Sagan as formative examples: people able to make the largest, most consequential ideas feel small enough to understand. The project is a love letter to that tradition of curiosity and public explanation.

Interpolations was the first executed activation of the brand: a design conference held in fall 2025 that brought together prominent leaders across design to discuss how AI is shaping the industry. Rusty is considering another edition in fall 2026.

## One case study now; possible split later

Rusty raised the concern that leading heavily with World's Fair history could hide the more immediate and substantial AI-conference story. The recommended current structure is one case study with unequal emphasis:

- I’ve Seen the Future supplies a brief personal frame in the opening and closing: the inherited phrase, Rusty's collection, and the long-term ambition to create public experiences around technology and the future.
- Interpolations occupies approximately 80–90 percent of the narrative as the actual project: its professional trigger, curatorial thesis, precarious formation, speakers, independent production, event, and consequences.
- The opening should reach the contemporary design/AI question within a few paragraphs. Do not spend a full preliminary section explaining World's Fair history before the reader knows a conference is coming.

If I’ve Seen the Future later produces distinct activations across science, architecture, space travel, or other future-facing subjects, it can become an umbrella landing page and Interpolations can become a child case study. Splitting them now would leave the umbrella page largely aspirational and disconnect the first activation from the personal idea that gave it meaning.

## A recurring practice of convening

Interpolations was an independent leap, but it was not Rusty's first experience creating or helping create gatherings.

- In college in New York, Rusty ran the Feast conference.
- He helped execute Skillshare's Penny Conference.
- He volunteered for the first two 99% Conferences.
- While working with CreativeMornings when it had only one chapter, Rusty wrote the first guide explaining how to create another chapter.
- In 2013, Rusty and his late partner created Nearly Impossible, a conference named after Edwin Land's observation that anything worth doing is “manifestly important and nearly impossible.” It convened founders and creative leaders while e-commerce, direct-to-consumer brands, and social media were still unsettled subjects. Speakers included the founders of Shopify and Warby Parker; one early panel asked whether brands should participate in social media.

Nearly Impossible is the most useful antecedent. It shows a recurring method: when a technological or cultural shift becomes important before a professional community has settled how to respond, Rusty creates a gathering where practitioners can compare what they know. The final case study needs only a concise reference, not this complete résumé. It should establish that Interpolations belongs to a practiced form of convening rather than narrating it as an inexperienced first attempt.

Rusty had also imagined Nearly Impossible growing toward something like a World's Fair, though it was never part of the I’ve Seen the Future brand. Keep that conceptual continuity separate from formal brand lineage.

## Brand-name origin

The name I’ve Seen the Future predates Interpolations by many years. Rusty derived it from the “I Have Seen the Future” pins associated with General Motors' Futurama attractions at the World's Fair. Rusty personally collects the original pins and owns many examples from the 1964 New York World's Fair.

Authoritative museum records confirm that the phrase and giveaway span both major New York fairs, rather than the 1930s Chicago fair Rusty tentatively remembered:

- At the 1939–1940 New York World's Fair, visitors leaving General Motors' “Highways and Horizons” pavilion and Norman Bel Geddes's original Futurama received pins reading “I Have Seen the Future.” The Smithsonian National Museum of American History holds multiple examples: `https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/nmah_1129256` and `https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_741597`.
- At the 1964–1965 New York World's Fair, General Motors revived both Futurama and the phrase. The Smithsonian holds a clip-on button catalogued as “I Have Seen the Future, General Motors Futurama”: `https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/nmah_1148738`.

The 1939 attraction is the historical origin; the 1964 Futurama II continued it. Rusty's personal objects are therefore strong original visual evidence. Prefer new photographs of his own collection over museum collection images, whose reuse conditions may be restricted.

This is not incidental trivia. The name connects Rusty's lifelong fascination with World's Fairs, his ambition to host one someday, his personal collection, and his interest in translating large technological possibilities into experiences ordinary people can enter and understand.

## Why Interpolations became necessary

The need emerged inside Rusty's professional and social world. He was personally excited by what was happening in AI and kept asking designers he respected how they were using it. Many had barely touched the tools, did not yet have an answer, or were asking Rusty for advice.

Rusty did not feel he had formed a complete enough opinion to advise them. What he did know was that he wanted to understand what other people were actually doing. Interpolations began as a way to put that question into a room: gather peers and leaders with different forms of experience, let them show and discuss their real practices, and allow the community to learn together rather than pretending one person already possessed the answer.

This makes the case study less about teaching a predetermined “AI playbook” than the current event-site copy suggests. The more personal and accurate premise is: Rusty had a question his professional community shared, so he created the conditions for people to answer it together.

## Why “Interpolations”

Rusty does not remember one precise naming moment. The word represented being in the middle of a shifting relationship among physical art, digital design, and generative technology—working between known points while the larger shape was still emerging.

The subject was intentionally broad: design and creativity rather than one software category. Speakers and attendees came from digital product design, fine art and museums, consumer products, branding, technology, and other creative disciplines.

Rusty imagined Interpolations as the art-and-design space within I’ve Seen the Future. Future activations might address science, architecture, space travel, or other subjects under their own sub-brands. Interpolations needed to feel credible among museums and New York's cultural and design communities. I’ve Seen the Future, by contrast, is ultimately intended to become more mass and public while retaining cultural credibility—“Chevy, Heartbeat of America” scale and openness rather than an insider-only art identity.

Do not over-explain this nascent brand architecture in the first case study. The useful idea is that Interpolations names a particular artistic lens within a much broader ambition.

## Rusty's ownership

Rusty owned the event essentially end to end. From the initial idea through the final thank-you and closing, he personally carried the concept, outreach, speaker recruitment, programming, venue booking, sponsorship or financial planning, ticketing, catering, travel, visual identity, website, physical production, hosting, and day-of execution. He cold-contacted every speaker and persuaded each to participate. He did this while living outside New York and flew in to produce the event.

The one specifically remembered production assist was from Rusty's fiancée, who helped make approximately half of the lanyards. Final credits should still identify any venue, catering, photography/video, sponsor, volunteer, speaker, or other production contributions once known, but the case study does not need to obscure that this was fundamentally Rusty's independent undertaking.

The repository's sole-author commit history therefore corroborates only one visible part of a much wider authorship story.

## Personal and financial stakes

The idea arrived during a personally uncertain period. Rusty was staying in an Airbnb in the desert, had almost no money available, and was trying to determine what to do next professionally. His excitement about AI and the knowledge gap among his peers felt like an opportunity, but there was no institution, budget, venue, or existing event operation behind it.

The event initially depended on a chain of individual yeses. Rusty believes any early refusal might have caused the idea to collapse. The first ticket sale mattered as evidence that someone would pay to enter the room. MagicPath's $10,000 sponsorship was the make-or-break commitment that let him treat the event as real before he had booked a venue. Rusty might have found another path had it fallen through, but at the time he did not know where the necessary money would otherwise come from.

This is powerful but sensitive personal context. The first public draft should translate it with restraint—for example, “I began without an institution, venue, or budget behind me during an uncertain professional moment”—and can name the $10,000 sponsorship as the commitment that activated production if useful. Do not publish “nothing in my bank account” or the full desert crisis scene without Rusty's explicit approval after seeing the draft.

## The chain of yeses

Rusty personally selected and cold-contacted every speaker.

Emmett Shine may have been the first speaker Rusty approached. Rusty had read Emmett's writing about AI and design and recognized it as exactly the conversation the event needed. Instead of tentatively agreeing, Emmett and Little Plains asked for a full hour because they had an entire show they wanted to present. That response helped the event feel real and began the relationship that later led to Rusty's role at Little Plains.

Kelli Anderson, already a friend, supplied a different kind of credibility. The agency and technology participants established professional relevance; Kelli made the lineup serious from the perspective of fine art, museums, and independent creative practice. Many attendees came specifically to see her. She sold advance copies of her then-unreleased book at the event and sold out of them.

MagicPath's sponsorship, Emmett's expansive yes, Kelli's participation, the first ticket purchase, and the remaining speaker commitments should be presented as a cumulative formation story rather than a frictionless lineup announcement. Each yes made the next one more plausible.

## What the day felt like

Rusty describes the day as “vibey,” unusually open, raw, and deliberately or effectively “unconferencey.” It was not highly polished, and that informality was part of what participants responded to. There were many talks, but the atmosphere allowed people to be candid rather than perform a finished corporate position on AI.

The repeated word is **raw**. The final narrative should explore whether that rawness was planned, an effect of one person producing everything, a reflection of the subject's unsettled state, or all three. It should not convert the event into a glossy conference-production story if its real value came from immediacy, openness, and unfinished thinking.

The final story should not devote an entire chronological section to a detailed run of show. Rusty's preferred emphasis is the work and effort required to make the gathering exist and the caliber and range of people it assembled. The event itself is an important beat and visual proof: a one-day sequence of talks, lunch, informal conversation, and people returning home.

The strongest description is simple. People sat together in a raw room. One speaker went onstage, challenged or amazed them, sat down, and the next did the same. There were no elaborate sponsor reels, performative social-media activations, or polished conference rituals competing with the exchange. Participants repeatedly complimented the openness and simplicity of the environment.

## The production gap that became part of the format

Rusty entered production wanting a high level of stagecraft, environmental design, physical artifacts, merchandise, and motion graphics. Those qualities had been resonant in Nearly Impossible, which Rusty produced with his late partner. He tried to carry some of that spirit himself by making lanyards and signs by hand.

The visual-production ambition did not land to Rusty's own standard. In retrospect, doing less and buying or specifying a smaller number of elements tastefully might have produced a more coherent result. Rusty also recognized the creative role his late partner had played in willing the visual and environmental experience of Nearly Impossible into existence. For another Interpolations, he would want a strong visual creative partner across set design, projection and motion, and physical production.

What Rusty experienced as a weakness did not read that way to participants. The unvarnished space made the event feel unlike a conventional sponsored conference. People could focus on one another and the ideas. The case study can hold both truths: the rawness generated a quality the audience valued, and Rusty still wants a more intentional visual system next time. Avoid manufacturing a near-disaster scene; the honest tension is the gap between visual ambition and available capacity.

## The answer Rusty left with

Rusty entered Interpolations unsure whether AI and the new creative technologies had anything genuinely substantial to offer the designers he considered among the world's best. He was interested himself, but did not place his own excitement on the same evidentiary level as their professional judgment.

The event resolved that question. Hearing leaders such as Randy Hunt and Liz Danzico describe how completely their teams and practices were already changing made the shift feel real. The speakers differed in how far ahead they were and how they were using the tools, but the collective answer was not indifference. They saw consequential opportunity and a need to understand it.

Rusty left believing he needed to learn AI more deeply: study it, use it, teach it, and lean into the emerging practice. In retrospect, the world described in the room in October 2025 became ordinary remarkably quickly. Re-running the same conversation now would begin from a much more advanced baseline. The event did not conclude the inquiry; it confirmed that the race to understand the new creative landscape had begun.

## Evidence that it worked

- The very first person to buy a ticket has already asked when the next Interpolations will happen.
- Speakers and attendees continue telling Rusty that the event was great and that he should produce another one.
- The most consequential personal outcome was Rusty's relationship with Emmett Shine. Rusty did not know Emmett before cold-contacting him for Interpolations. That connection ultimately led to Rusty's role at Little Plains and his work helping create the studio's new website.
- Rusty is considering another Interpolations in fall 2026, but no future edition should be represented as announced until he says it is public.

Avoid reducing success to the Little Plains job; it is compelling proof that the gathering generated real relationships, but the event's value also lies in the participants' continued enthusiasm and desire to reconvene.

## Visual identity and event artifacts

Rusty created the portal illustration, wordmark, and overall visual identity himself. The portal image was generated using either Midjourney or ChatGPT; exact tool/provenance can be recovered later if useful. Rusty assembled the identity in Figma and coded the site.

Rusty does not consider the identity among his strongest work and does not want the case study to overstate it. The visual edit should therefore use the identity as connective tissue or historical context rather than treating it as the project's main design achievement. Real evidence from the gathering—people, room, talks, handmade lanyards, signage, process, and motion—will likely carry more emotional and editorial weight.

Event video and other artifacts exist. They require some polishing but can be used. Their location, rights, credits, and condition still need to be established.

## Explicit exclusions

- The Creative AI Tool Directory was not part of the event experience in any meaningful way. Rusty built it over one weekend, never promoted it, and reports that effectively nobody saw it. Exclude it from the case study rather than using it as evidence of an extended educational platform.
- Attendee-portal prototypes likewise have no place in the narrative unless later firsthand evidence establishes that they affected the real event.

## Scope decision

- I’ve Seen the Future is the durable platform and personal motivation.
- Interpolations is its first major public expression and the concrete center of the first case study.
- This should not become a case study about a software lab, collection of AI tools, or private experimentation environment.
- The current personal-site metadata is incorrect and will eventually need replacement: it describes “a working lab for learning, testing, and organizing creative AI tools.”

## Provisional thesis

> I’ve Seen the Future is Rusty's long-term platform for making consequential ideas about technology feel understandable, inviting, and public. Its first expression was Interpolations: when Rusty realized that neither he nor many designers around him had a settled answer for how AI would change their practice, he built the room in which they could begin answering it together.

Possible narrative contrast:

> The ambition is eventually a World's Fair. The first step was one raw, open room in New York.

Refined current structure:

1. **Open in the present problem, with the pin close at hand.** Identify Interpolations as a design-and-AI conference immediately, then use the vintage phrase to explain the larger I’ve Seen the Future frame without delaying the project.
2. **I did not have the answer.** Rusty's peers were asking what AI meant for creative practice; he shared the question rather than pretending to possess doctrine.
3. **A familiar method for an unfamiliar shift.** One compact Nearly Impossible reference establishes that Rusty has repeatedly convened people around emerging professional change.
4. **A chain of yeses.** No initial budget or institution; MagicPath, Emmett, Kelli, the first ticket, and the full speaker group progressively make the event possible.
5. **Everything required to make one room exist.** Rusty's end-to-end role, geographic and logistical effort, and the visual ambition/capacity tension.
6. **It happened.** A compact, image-led section on the raw room and simple format rather than a detailed agenda recap.
7. **The answer from the room.** The design leaders' conviction changes Rusty's own path toward AI; the gathering also creates durable relationships, including Little Plains.
8. **The first activation, not the whole future.** Return briefly to I’ve Seen the Future and the possibility of other domains and future gatherings.

This is promising but should not be treated as final copy until Rusty confirms how prominently he wants the World's Fair ambition stated.
