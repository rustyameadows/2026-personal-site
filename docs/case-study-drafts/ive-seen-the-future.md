## I didn’t have the answer, so I built the room

In October 2025, I produced Interpolations, a one-day conference in New York about design and AI. Ten artists, designers, founders, educators, and creative leaders took the stage in a raw room to talk about how their work was changing—and what they thought was coming next.

I made the conference because I had a question I could not answer alone.

I was personally excited by what was happening with AI, so I kept asking designers I respected how they were using it. Many had barely touched the tools. Others were asking me what they should try. I had opinions, but not enough conviction to pretend I had a playbook for everyone else.

What I did recognize was the opportunity inside our shared uncertainty. If the people around me did not yet know what AI meant for creative practice, perhaps the useful thing was not another prediction. It was a room where people with genuinely different experiences could compare what they were seeing.

I called it Interpolations: a gathering about the changing space among physical art, digital design, technology, and creative work. It became the first public expression of a much older project of mine called I’ve Seen the Future.

## A pin from somebody else’s future

The name I’ve Seen the Future predates Interpolations by years. It comes from the “I Have Seen the Future” pins handed to visitors leaving General Motors' Futurama at the New York World's Fairs; I collect the 1964 pins.

What has stayed with me is the fairs' ability to turn difficult technology into a public experience people could enter and understand—an impulse I also found in Richard Feynman and Carl Sagan, who could make the largest ideas feel intimate enough to hold.

I’ve Seen the Future is my long-term container for that ambition. I want it to explore technology, science, design, architecture, space, and whatever else is arriving before most of us have the language for it. Interpolations was its first public expression: the art-and-design room inside a much larger idea.

The larger ambition may be a World's Fair. The first step was one room in New York.

## A familiar method for an unfamiliar shift

Interpolations was not my first conference. In 2013, my late partner and I created Nearly Impossible, bringing founders and designers together while e-commerce, direct-to-consumer brands, and social media were still unsettled questions. One early panel asked whether brands should participate in social media at all.

I recognized the pattern. A consequential shift had arrived before the profession had found shared language for it. Sometimes the most useful thing I can make is the context in which other people can exchange what they know.

## The event began as a chain of yeses

I did not begin with an institution, a venue, or an event budget behind me. The idea arrived during an uncertain professional moment, and initially the conference could have disappeared after almost any early no.

So I started asking.

I selected and personally contacted every speaker. I had been reading Emmett Shine's writing about AI and design and thought: this is exactly the conversation we need to have. We did not know one another. When I invited him, he and the Little Plains team did not cautiously agree to a short talk; they asked whether they could have a full hour because they had an entire show they wanted to put on.

That was one of the moments the idea began to feel real.

Kelli Anderson, a friend whose work connected the gathering to fine-art and museum audiences, broadened the lineup in an entirely different direction. Agencies and technology companies could demonstrate that AI mattered to the business of creative work. Kelli's participation told artists and educators that the room was also for them. Attendees told me they had come to see her, and the advance copies of her then-unreleased book sold out.

MagicPath agreed to sponsor the event before I had booked the venue. That commitment gave the project a real budget and moved it from an idea I might still abandon into something I had to produce. The first person who bought a ticket provided another kind of proof: somebody outside the speaker list wanted this room enough to pay for a seat in it.

Each yes changed the odds of the next one. By the time the lineup was complete, it included Kelli Anderson, Ted Galperin, Emmett Shine, Randy Hunt, Liz Danzico, Oscar Dumlao, Derrick Schultz, Pietro Schirano, Raul Gutierrez, and Dan Batten—people approaching creativity from fine art, museums, education, agencies, products, AI companies, and independent practice.

## Everything required to make one room exist

I made essentially every part of Interpolations myself.

I developed the premise and name, recruited the speakers, found the sponsors, booked the venue and catering, set the program, sold the tickets, arranged the travel, designed the identity and website, made the physical materials, communicated with everyone involved, hosted the gathering, and handled the day-of production. I did not live in New York. At the end, I flew there and made the thing happen.

My fiancée helped make about half of the lanyards. That was approximately the size of the production department.

I wanted the environmental detail that had made Nearly Impossible memorable, much of it made possible by the visual force of my late creative partner. I made lanyards and signs by hand, but without a dedicated visual partner I spread myself too thin. Next time I would do less, make a smaller physical system more coherent, and give the stage, projections, and motion design their own owner.

What felt under-produced to me had an unexpected effect in the room. People repeatedly said how good it felt to be somewhere raw. There was no glossy sponsor reel, no obligatory social-media activation, and little of the theater that can make a conference feel like a trade show about itself. People sat together. One person walked onstage and expanded the room's sense of what was possible, then the next person did it again.

The rawness was partly a constraint. It also became the format.

## It happened

Interpolations lasted one day. There were talks, lunch, conversation, and then everybody went home.

That simplicity is important. The accomplishment was not an elaborate format. It was bringing this particular group together early enough that their uncertainty and conviction were both still visible.

I entered the event wondering whether AI had something real to offer the designers I considered among the best in the world. I was excited by it, but I did not consider my own interest sufficient proof. I wanted to know whether these people were embracing the technology, changing their practices, and seeing a future worth taking seriously.

The answer from the room was yes.

Hearing leaders such as Randy Hunt and Liz Danzico describe how differently their teams were already working made the shift concrete. Other speakers were ahead in different ways: building tools, training models, experimenting through art, or reorganizing creative processes. They did not present one unified method, but indifference was no longer a plausible conclusion. The world of creative work was changing, and the race to understand it had begun.

I left believing I needed to learn AI in a way I had not before: to study it, use it, teach it, and lean into it. Less than a year later, much of the world the speakers described already feels ordinary. If we reconvened the same people now, the conversation would begin from a dramatically more advanced baseline.

The conference did not answer every question. It convinced me the questions were worth organizing part of my life around.

## What left the room

The first person who bought a ticket has already asked when the next Interpolations will happen. Speakers and attendees still tell me that the day was special and that we should do it again.

The event also changed my own path in a way I could not have planned. Before I cold-emailed Emmett Shine, we had never met. The relationship that began through Interpolations ultimately led to my work with Little Plains, where I helped turn the studio's long-running website ambition into the product it launched the following year.

That job is not the measure of whether the gathering succeeded, but it is proof of what a real room can produce. Interpolations created relationships, changed what I chose to study, and gave a latent personal project its first public form.

I’ve Seen the Future is intended to be larger than one design conference. Interpolations can continue as its space for art, design, and creative technology; other subjects may eventually require their own names and gatherings. The goal is not to predict the future correctly from a distance. It is to make consequential change understandable enough that people can enter the conversation themselves.

It started with one raw room, ten remarkable speakers, and people asking when we would do it again. That is enough of a future to keep building toward—and a beginning for the promise on the pin that I am still trying to live up to.
