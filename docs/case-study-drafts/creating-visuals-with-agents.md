## Watching one model use another

I like playing with image generation. What pulled me much further into it, though, was discovering how good text and reasoning models are at using image-generation models themselves.

When I use an image model directly, some amount of hope is always involved. I describe what I want, generate something, revise the prompt, and try again. That wandering can be fun, and plenty of great work comes out of it. But it can also feel like firing shotguns into the air and waiting to see what falls down.

Watching an agent operate the same model feels different.

I can give Codex or GPT an idea and watch it develop a concept document, break the direction into twenty-four independent prompts, run them in parallel, and review the entire field. If the work misses, the agent can often identify how its own instruction was unclear. It rewrites the weak part, narrows the next pass, and tries again. It does not feel like it is hoping the image model understands. It feels like it knows how to use the tool.

Then it can keep working. It can cut images apart, remove backgrounds, align them, normalize them, recolor them, composite them, build comparison boards, sequence frames, test timing, and turn generated material into animation or a working interface. The conversation can move from a loose idea through image generation, code, and production without requiring me to manually translate every decision from one tool into the next.

I have access to the same image models. The surprising capability is the agent sitting above them: its ability to turn intent into a coordinated exploration, look at what happened, understand what needs to change, and continue.

This page is a growing collection of things I have made that way.

## Starting from nothing

Every experiment here started with me at a blank spot.

Sometimes I begin with references. Sometimes I mark up an image and point to what bothers me. Sometimes I know the feeling but have no idea what form it should take. Sometimes the whole direction begins with a passing question and becomes much larger than I expected.

I talk to the agent, respond to what comes back, reject things, redirect it, and keep grinding. The agent can turn that conversation into a volume of coordinated visual work that would otherwise take far longer. It can make many things without making the same thing many times. It can hold onto a palette, silhouette, timing behavior, reference, or production constraint while exploring around it.

The projects do not share a visual language, and they do not need one. A moving watercolor sphere, an interactive color swatch, an editorial illustration, and a synthetic model are completely different things. The connection is simply that I made each of them by working with an agent.

Some became production assets. Some became huge archives. Some were side experiments I barely showed anyone. Some are finished, some are unresolved, and some are still happening.

They can all live here.

## Orbs

Orbs began with three visual references from different artists and a loose idea about painted color moving around an implied sphere.

From there, I spent round after round telling the agent what I wanted, looking at what it made, and pushing it somewhere else. The work moved through image generation, local manipulation, compositing, animation, different speeds, timing systems, color transfer, registration, and behavioral studies. One visual idea expanded into hundreds of moving studies and thousands of retained artifacts.

That range is what makes Orbs important here. It is not that I consider every final orb perfect, or even that the current presentation is the final presentation I want. It is the experience of directing an agent through a visual idea while it moves fluently among generation, analysis, image processing, code, and motion.

The agent was not only producing pictures for me to choose from. It was helping build an entire environment for exploring them: making variations, applying transformations, comparing results, testing how they moved, and carrying selected material into increasingly elaborate compositions.

The full Orbs archive preserves the detailed experiment from its references through R00–R12. This page does not need to repeat every turn. It can show the breadth of the work and let anyone who wants the complete record fall through into it.

## Little Plains watercolors

The Little Plains watercolors began much more casually.

I was working on a set of color controls. First they were dots. Then they were Xs. Then I asked, “What about watercolors?”

The agent and I started exploring. We moved through different families of painted marks, selected a direction, generated each color, developed the way pigment should move inside the forms, and processed the results into working animation assets.

The important thing is that they did not remain image-generation studies. They went into production.

The final system contains twelve individually made watercolor controls, each with sixteen baked painted states. That is 192 frames of generated and processed material, assembled into interactive controls on the Little Plains website. Their resting frames remain exact. Their transparency was cleaned. Colors that drifted were corrected without blurring or regenerating them. The controls load safely, animate when appropriate, return to their resting state, respect reduced-motion preferences, and fall back cleanly if an animation cannot load.

That production work is part of what excites me about creating visuals with agents. An offhand idea did not stop at a mood board or an attractive sheet of watercolor marks. The same conversation could continue through visual exploration, generation, image processing, motion, implementation, and all the small corrections required to make the work real.

I asked whether the controls could be watercolors. Then we made the watercolors, made them move, and shipped them.

## Editorial illustrations

I am now using ChatGPT and Codex to create illustrations for Little Plains's article about its new website.

That work asks for something different. The images have to live beside language, clarify ideas, support the studio's voice, and make a technical story feel inviting. I can give the agent the article, discuss the moments that need illustration, explore several possible visual ideas, and develop whichever directions actually belong next to the writing.

The resulting work does not need to resemble Orbs or the watercolor controls. That is part of the point. I am not using the agent to manufacture one repeatable aesthetic. I am using it to move through different visual problems and make different kinds of things.

## Building a cast

In an earlier experiment, I generated an entire modeling agency because I wanted better models available for image-making.

The project was less about making one perfect finished image than creating a cast I could draw from. That idea has stayed with me. Image generation can make final pictures, but it can also make ingredients: people, props, materials, places, gestures, and visual worlds that can be returned to and recombined.

I have been thinking about doing the same thing with props. A great prop stylist has an accumulated world of objects and knows what can be pulled into a scene. A generated library could become another kind of creative inventory—not something that has to be materialized from nothing every time a model needs to make an image.

The modeling-agency work will join this page if it still feels strong when I return to the archive. The larger idea is already part of how I think about these tools: generation can build the components of a visual practice, not only its final outputs.

## A collection that can keep changing

I want to push image generation much further in the next Interpolations. Explore Art makes me want to experiment with teaser films and transitions that move through different artworks. I am still interested in building a prop library. There will be other things I cannot name yet.

They do not need to converge into one project or style.

The common experience is that I can begin in conversation and stay there while the work moves through increasingly complicated forms of execution. I can describe an intention, react to what I see, and let an agent coordinate the enormous space between the idea and the artifact.

Sometimes the result is a strange side experiment. Sometimes it is hundreds of animations. Sometimes it becomes a tiny production detail that thousands of people can touch on a website.

I made all of it by talking to an agent. I still find that completely fascinating.
