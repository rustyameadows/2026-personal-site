## Building a search engine for looking at art

Explore Art is a search engine for moving through art by what you see, remember, or wonder about.

You can describe an atmosphere in ordinary language. You can begin with an artwork and ask for something visually related. You can click a particular blue in a painting's palette and find the same color in works from other periods and collections. You can select one small region inside an image and search from that detail. When looking turns into a question, you can ask about the work, its artist, its context, or its relationship to something else.

The current site is a first version, but the subject is not new for me. I studied art history, worked in a museum for four years, and once curated a Warhol exhibition with institutional loans, previously unseen material, and speakers connected directly to his life and methods. After moving to New York, I built an earlier product called Discover Art to help people find exhibitions across the city's museums.

Museum work turned out not to be for me. I kept returning to the same product anyway: a better way for people to find art and follow their curiosity through it.

Explore Art is the most ambitious version I have made so far.

## The open corpus I thought I was building

The project started with an infrastructure idea.

I had built several products with multimodal embeddings and noticed that I kept caching the same results. Within a fixed model and embedding space, a phrase does not need to be converted into a vector again every time a small application wants to use it. I began talking through an idea with ChatGPT Pro: what if there were an open corpus of the most useful visual phrases around art and design?

It might include ten thousand concepts covering materials, movements, compositions, moods, subjects, and terms related to systems such as the Art Genome. A developer could download those vectors instead of making the same calls again. The corpus could also include canonical artworks. If somebody wanted to compare an archive against the Mona Lisa, they could begin with a known compatible reference instead of locating, verifying, and embedding another copy themselves.

I liked the idea of a shared visual vocabulary. The more I developed it, however, the harder it became to imagine raw vectors being the thing people would actually use.

An embedding is only one ingredient. I still needed to determine which image represents a work, whether it can be used, where it came from, how to reconcile museum metadata, which relationships are facts, and how exact constraints should behave. Then I needed an interface people would actually want to use.

The corpus stopped being the product. I decided to use it as the foundation for the best art-discovery experience I could build.

## Making it a real product

Art and vector search fit together naturally, and there have been plenty of demos. The examples I found usually covered a small, selected set of works created for a model launch or museum collaboration.

Then the demo ends. The institution's full catalog, real search, source records, rights, ingestion, and public website remain somewhere else.

I wanted the same product model to hold whether Explore Art contained hundreds of works or a million. Scaling should require more resources, not a rebuild. That meant treating operational concerns as part of the experiment from the beginning.

The first catalog is small and mixed on purpose. I could have filled it from one convenient collection, but I wanted to test the system against different APIs, metadata, image behavior, and rights language. Every new source breaks a different assumption.

The application continuously moves individual works through source archival, rights review, image processing, embeddings, visual analysis, checking, and publication. PostgreSQL is the source of truth for what is live. The vector index can be rebuilt. A failed artwork can stay unpublished without blocking the rest.

It is more infrastructure than a throwaway demo needs because I wanted the experiment to keep working if the catalog grew.

## Search by what you mean

Museum search is usually strongest when you already know the title, artist, period, or accession language. Explore Art is designed for the less settled beginning: a half-memory, visual quality, feeling, material, gesture, or comparison.

Natural-language search handles that open-ended part. Embeddings can match a phrase to appearance and meaning even when the museum record never uses those words. Exact facts still stay exact: artist, institution, date, medium, geography, and checked taxonomy. Boolean relationships remain as written, and an empty intersection stays empty.

The rule is simple: structured evidence filters the catalog, then semantic similarity ranks the results.

A person can begin with a loose description, add an exact artist or color, combine terms, and keep editing in the same search field. Curiosity can become more precise without requiring a separate advanced-search tool.

## Color became a way through the collection

Color search is the part that most consistently delights me.

Each suitable artwork receives a deterministic palette extracted from its normalized image. I save those colors as search data. Click one blue in an artwork and Explore Art finds works carrying perceptually related colors, then shows the matching chips in the results.

The effect can feel improbable. The paintings or objects may have nothing obvious in common, yet the exact dusty blue or saturated red passes through all of them. A tiny piece of one artwork becomes a path across institutions, centuries, subjects, and media.

I also built a color naming system that makes the swatches easier to scan than hex codes alone, while the measured color still drives the search.

I am still comparing palette methods, and I do not think the extraction is solved. The interaction already works well enough to be exciting, even though the algorithm is still a V0.

## Search inside an artwork

Sometimes the thing you want to search for is only one part of an artwork.

On a detail page, you can draw a box around a face in a crowd, a fragment of pattern, the shape of a hand, or a small figure near the horizon. Explore Art searches from that crop, and the same selection can become context for a question.

Now discovery can start with the specific hand, face, pattern, or figure someone noticed. Future segmentation could make identified figures, objects, surfaces, backgrounds, gestures, and relationships searchable too.

Ask Art lets the same exploration continue in language. Art history is well documented and changes slowly, so even this minimally guided version is already useful for questions about an artist, period, technique, iconography, or comparison. The agent receives the current artwork and stored evidence as context.

I want to give it stronger art-historical guidance, better research paths, more useful comparisons, and closer links between explanation and visual discovery. The current version already makes it easy to move between looking and asking.

## Rights before resolution

My art-history background made rights part of the foundation from day one.

Studying and exhibiting Warhol taught me how difficult modern-art clearance can become. I did not want to build a compelling product and then discover that its underlying corpus made the whole experiment unusable.

Explore Art distinguishes an artwork from the particular image that represents it. It preserves the source, rights statement, attribution, and other evidence for that representation. A work becomes public only when the image and its required analysis are ready. Source and rights remain visible on the artwork page.

The present catalog is a baby version, including its image resolution. Starting with images we can legitimately use means future versions can add higher-resolution viewing, better crops, segmentation, palettes, and new visual experiences without reopening the rights question each time.

## Building with an agent at product scale

I originated, designed, and built Explore Art without human collaborators. ChatGPT Pro was the early conceptual partner. Codex became my primary implementation collaborator across the Rails application, ingestion, retrieval, interface, evaluation, operations, and deployment. Gemini supplies the multimodal embedding space; other model-backed systems analyze the artwork and power Ask Art.

The first build took about two weeks, after several days of working through the concept and years of related experiments. I could move that quickly because this was not my first embedding pipeline, art-discovery idea, color system, or product. The agent helped me turn all of that prior work into a much more complete first version.

The clearest example came when I wanted to send a working link to a friend just as I had to leave town for three days. Until then, the application and its corpus lived on my computer. I left the machine running, gave Codex scoped access to Render, gave it a budget and a clear deployment goal, and enabled the computer access needed to complete it.

Over roughly an hour, the agent deployed the application and moved the local corpus into hosted PostgreSQL. Connecting the repository was only the first step. It also had to migrate the data and bring the remote system online. I checked in periodically while traveling, and by the end Explore Art had a public address.

That was the first time I watched an agent take on an operational job of that size. I set the goal and limits, checked in while I was traveling, and let it work through the deployment.

## Everything is V0

Explore Art is live, but I do not consider it finished.

The palettes can improve. The Art Genome was one taxonomy I could implement quickly, not necessarily the permanent organizing system. I am still deciding whether the catalog should favor paintings and two-dimensional visual work for cleaner search or expand further into objects and historical artifacts. I want to add other public visual corpora, including the Library of Congress and National Parks archives. Region selection should grow into segmentation. Ask Art has barely begun to receive the expert guidance it deserves.

The real test is whether designers, art historians, and other curious people actually use it.

The same foundation could support an ambient art screensaver, a playful history-of-art maker, an Eames-inspired *Powers of Ten* through visual relationships, or a generated history of cerulean across centuries of images. Search is the first thing I built on top of it.

I have made versions of this product for years as the technology changed. Explore Art is the first one that feels capable of becoming the product I kept trying to make: a place where one thing leads to another and people want to keep looking.
