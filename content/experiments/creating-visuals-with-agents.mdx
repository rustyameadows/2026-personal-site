## Watching one model use another

I like playing with image generation. What pulled me much further into it, though, was discovering how good text and reasoning models are at using image-generation models themselves.

When I use an image model directly, some amount of hope is always involved. I describe what I want, generate something, revise the prompt, and try again. That wandering can be fun, and plenty of great work comes out of it. But it can also feel like firing shotguns into the air and waiting to see what falls down.

Watching an agent operate the same model feels different.

I can give Codex or GPT an idea and watch it develop a concept document, split the direction into twenty-four independent prompts, run them in parallel, and review the results. When something misses, the agent can usually tell which part of its own instruction was unclear, rewrite it, and try again. It seems to understand how to use the image model instead of just hoping for a lucky result.

Then it can keep going. It can remove backgrounds, composite, recolor, build comparison boards, or sequence frames. The same conversation can carry an idea from generation into code, motion, and a working interface.

I have access to the same image models. What I am exploring here is what changes when an agent directs them, evaluates the results, and keeps working.

This page is a growing collection of things I have made that way.

## Starting from nothing

Every experiment here started with me at a blank spot.

I might begin with references, an image marked up with what bothers me, a feeling with no form yet, or a passing question that grows into something much bigger.

I talk to the agent, respond to what comes back, reject things, redirect it, and keep grinding. It can generate a lot of work without collapsing into near-duplicates, while keeping track of a palette, silhouette, timing behavior, reference, or production constraint.

The work does not share a visual language. The only connection between a moving watercolor sphere, an interactive color swatch, an editorial illustration, and a synthetic model is that I made each one by talking to an agent.

Some became production assets or huge archives. Others are side experiments I barely showed anyone. A few are finished. Plenty are unresolved or still happening.

## Orbs

Orbs began with three visual references from different artists and a loose idea about painted color moving around an implied sphere.

From there, I spent round after round telling the agent what I wanted, looking at what it made, and pushing it somewhere else. The work moved through image generation, local editing, compositing, animation, and many studies of timing and color. One visual idea expanded into hundreds of moving studies and thousands of retained artifacts.

What interests me about Orbs is the range. I am less attached to any one final image than I am to the experience of directing an agent that could move with me through generation, analysis, image processing, code, and motion.

The agent also helped build the environment around the pictures. It made variations, applied transformations, compared results, tested movement, and carried selected material into more elaborate compositions.

The full Orbs archive traces the experiment from its references through rounds R00 to R12. It holds the complete record. Here I am showing the breadth of the work.

## Little Plains watercolors

The Little Plains watercolors began much more casually.

I was working on a set of color controls. First they were dots. Then they were Xs. Then I asked, “What about watercolors?”

The agent and I started exploring. We moved through different families of painted marks, selected a direction, generated each color, developed the way pigment should move inside the forms, and processed the results into working animation assets.

Little Plains shipped the results on its website.

The final system has twelve watercolor controls, each with sixteen painted states, for a total of 192 generated and processed frames. I cleaned their transparency, corrected drifting colors without blurring or regenerating them, and locked the resting frames. The controls animate when appropriate, respect reduced-motion preferences, and fall back cleanly if an animation fails.

That is what excites me about this way of working. An offhand idea became a real interface detail because the agent could stay with the work through image processing, animation, implementation, and cleanup.

I asked whether the controls could be watercolors. Then we made the watercolors, made them move, and shipped them.

## Editorial illustrations

I am now using ChatGPT and Codex to create illustrations for Little Plains's article about its new website.

Those images have to support the article, clarify its ideas, and fit the studio's voice. I can give the agent the draft, decide which moments need an image, explore several ideas, and develop the directions that work beside the writing.

The results can look nothing like Orbs or the watercolor controls. I want the agent to help me solve different visual problems, not stamp everything with one aesthetic.

## Building a cast

In an earlier experiment, I generated an entire modeling agency because I wanted better models available for image-making.

The project gave me a cast I could return to for future images. That idea has stayed with me. Image generation can make ingredients such as people, props, materials, places, gestures, and visual worlds that can be reused and recombined.

I have been thinking about doing the same thing with props. A great prop stylist has an accumulated world of objects and knows what can be pulled into a scene. A generated prop library could become that kind of reusable creative inventory.

The modeling-agency work will join this page if it still feels strong when I return to the archive. It already shapes how I use these tools: generation can build the people, props, and other pieces of a visual practice before it produces a final image.

## A collection that can keep changing

I want to push image generation much further in the next Interpolations. Explore Art makes me want to experiment with teaser films and transitions that move through different artworks. I am still interested in building a prop library. There will be other things I cannot name yet.

I do not expect all of it to converge into one project or style.

What connects the work is that I can stay in conversation while the agent moves from prompt writing into image generation, editing, code, and motion. I describe what I want, react to what I see, and let it coordinate the tools.

The results range from strange side experiments to hundreds of animations to tiny production details people can touch on a website.

I made all of it by talking to an agent. I still find that completely fascinating.
